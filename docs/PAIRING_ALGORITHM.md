## The quality vector

B.8 says one candidate beats another if it better satisfies a higher-priority
criterion, and that equal candidates are separated by which was generated first.
That is a lexicographic comparison, so each candidate is reduced to a vector of
numbers where lower is better, laid out in the rules' own order:

```
[ C.5, …PSD (C.6), C.7 ×2, C.8, C.9, C.10, C.11,
  C.12, C.13, C.14, C.15, C.16, C.17, C.18, C.19 ]
```

Two blocks vary in length. Both are preceded by the criterion that fixes that
length — C.5 fixes how many score differences there are, and C.12–C.15 fix how
many float repeats there are — so by the time a comparison reaches either block,
the two candidates already agree on its size.

Two deliberate choices inside this:

**PSD as bucket counts.** A.8 defines the PSD as the sorted list of score
differences, compared lexicographically. The search instead counts how many
differences fall in each distinct value, largest value first. For lists of equal
length the two orderings are identical, and the count form has the property the
search needs: adding a pair can only increase a count, never reorder the vector.

**C.16–C.19 as totals.** The rules say "minimize the score differences" of the
players repeating a float, without saying whether to compare the collection
element by element or in total. This uses the total, because a total is monotone
under adding pairs and can therefore be bounded. With C.12–C.15 already equal by
the time these are reached, the two readings agree in every case that arises.

## Making the search finish

A twenty-player homogeneous bracket has 10! orderings of S2, and exchanges
multiply that. Three things bring it down.

### Absolute criteria prune subtrees

Candidates are generated by a depth-first walk that fills S1's positions in turn,
choosing the lowest unused S2 player first — which produces exactly D.1's
ascending lexicographic order. Because the walk is incremental, a rematch (C.1)
or a forbidden colour clash (C.3) is caught at the node where it first appears
and removes every candidate beneath it at once.

### Branch and bound on the quality criteria

Every entry of the quality vector from C.5 to C.19 is a total that placing more
pairs can only raise. So a partial pairing already carries a lower bound on what
any completion of it can score, and if that bound loses to the best candidate
found so far, the subtree is abandoned.

The bound has to be admissible or pruning would discard winners. Two details make
it so:

- Score differences not yet decided are credited to the **smallest** bucket, the
  best they could possibly turn out to be. This also keeps every vector the same
  total size, so a three-pair partial is comparable with a twelve-pair candidate.
- The C.7 slots are left at zero, their floor.

Ties are pruned as well as losses. A candidate that merely equals the incumbent
cannot displace it, because B.8 awards a tie to whichever was generated first and
the incumbent always was.

### The colour criterion is solved rather than searched

C.10 — how many players are refused their colour preference — is what usually
decides a bracket, and it is the one criterion the search is worst at. Finding a
good candidate is easy; *proving* no better one exists means exhausting the
space.

But C.10 is a bipartite assignment problem. Each S1 player must take an S2
partner; the cost of a pairing is how many of the two it leaves with the wrong
colour, with forbidden pairs priced out of reach. The Hungarian algorithm answers
it exactly in O(n³), before the walk starts.

That answer is used three ways:

1. as a **hard target** — a partial pairing that has already refused more than the
   minimum is dropped immediately;
2. as the **C.10 entry of the bracket's quality floor**, so that a candidate
   meeting the floor is recognised as perfect and accepted at once (B.4);
3. as a **fallback pairing**, so a bracket too large to search still returns a
   legal, colour-optimal result instead of nothing.

The effect is large. The adversarial twenty-four-player bracket in the test suite
— every S1 player wanting the same colour as the S2 player opposite them — went
from spending a 120,000-node budget and settling for two refusals, to finding the
zero-refusal optimum after fourteen candidates.

If the interaction with the other criteria makes that minimum unreachable, the
target is relaxed a step at a time until the bracket pairs.

### A further bound on the pairs not yet placed

Even with a target, the walk needs to know when a partial pairing is doomed.
Every pair hands out exactly one white and one black, so if the players still to
be paired contain more white-wanters than there are pairs left, the surplus must
be refused whatever happens. That surplus is added to the partial's C.10 total
before it is compared, which is what lets a bad first choice be abandoned at
depth one instead of at depth twelve.

## Completing the round

Brackets are paired from the top down, each inheriting the previous one's
downfloaters. After each one, the engine asks whether the round can still be
finished — and this is subtler than it looks. It is not enough to check that a
maximum matching exists over the remaining players and that *some* player is
eligible for the bye: in an odd field, the one player a maximum matching leaves
out may be precisely the one who has already had a bye. So the question is asked
the other way round — is there an eligible player whose removal leaves everyone
else pairable?

Getting this wrong was a real bug during development, and it is what the
`missedCompletions` check in the test suite exists to catch.

When the answer is no, A.9's collapse applies: the bracket just paired becomes
the Penultimate Pairing Bracket, every scoregroup below it merges into one
collapsed scoregroup, and the PPB is re-paired under C.4 with its downfloaters
constrained to a set that lets the round finish. Checking that constraint means a
matching over everyone still unpaired, far too expensive to repeat per candidate,
so it is memoised on the set of downfloaters — which is what the answer actually
depends on.

## What `exhaustive` means

`exhaustive: false` on a result means the pairing is legal and every absolute
rule holds, but it was not proved to be the one the rules' ordering picks. It is
set when a bracket hit its node budget without either finishing the walk or
reaching the computed floor. The app surfaces it rather than presenting the
pairing as unqualified, because an arbiter is entitled to know the difference.

`complete: false` is the serious one: no legal round-pairing exists at all, and
C.04.3.A.9 hands the decision to the arbiter.

## The two graph algorithms

Both are in `util/combinatorics.ts`, implemented from scratch.

**Edmonds' blossom algorithm** finds a maximum matching in a general graph. It is
needed because Swiss compatibility graphs are not bipartite: any two players may
be pairable. The complication general graphs bring is odd cycles — an augmenting
path can enter one and leave by a different vertex — which the algorithm handles
by contracting the cycle to a single node and continuing on the smaller graph.
Used for the completion check and the C.7 lookahead.

**The Hungarian algorithm** with potentials solves the assignment problem above in
O(n²m), handling the rectangular case where S2 is larger than S1.
